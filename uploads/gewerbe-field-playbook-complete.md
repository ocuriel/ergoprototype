# Commercial Lines Field Playbook — Germany (Complete Edition)
## Industries, coverages, need-spotting, and objection handling for the SMB market

A working and learning tool for the commercial SMB conversation, written for agents who are still building product knowledge. Every coverage in every industry is explained in plain language for that industry, carries a signal that tells you when to raise it, and carries a claim example drawn from that industry — the story you can retell to the client. Nothing is assumed as known.

**How the tiers work**

- **Must-have** — bind before the doors open. Includes covers that are legally or contractually mandatory (motor liability per vehicle, professional indemnity where professional law requires it) and the covers no serious advisor lets a client operate without.
- **Recommended** — raise on every qualifying account. The natural second and third policies in the bundle.
- **Situational** — know the cue, bring it up when it appears.

**Standing rules on every account, regardless of industry**

1. Underinsurance (Unterversicherung) is a standard check item on every document. Confirm sums insured against current values, and confirm whether a waiver of underinsurance (Unterversicherungsverzicht) and a premium-free buffer (Vorsorge) apply in the current terms — and at what level.
2. Never quote a specific sum insured, sublimit, deductible, or premium from memory. Name the feature, then confirm the figure in the current policy conditions (AVB) or tariff documentation. Coverage scope changes by tariff generation. All euro amounts in the claim examples below are illustrative loss sizes for storytelling, not policy figures.
3. § 19 VVG (pre-contractual disclosure) and § 6/§ 61 VVG (advice and documentation duty) are not compliance overhead — the documented advice record (Beratungsprotokoll) is a trust artifact in this market. Use it visibly.
4. Statutory accident insurance through the Berufsgenossenschaft covers employees' workplace and commuting accidents and occupational diseases automatically. It is never something to sell — but its boundaries (leisure time, benefit level, the owner personally) are where the supplementary conversation legitimately starts.

# Part 1 — The 16 industry groupings

## 1. Construction & Finishing Trades (Bau- und Ausbauhandwerk)
**Typical businesses:** Electrical, plumbing/heating/climate (SHK), carpentry & joinery, tile & screed, painting & drywall, roofing & scaffolding
**Opening question:** "Who is on the hook if your work causes a loss months after handover?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht with a construction wording** — pays when the firm's work injures someone or damages someone else's property. For the trades, the decisive parts are the clauses most basic policies restrict: damage to the very thing being worked on (Bearbeitungs-/Tätigkeitsschäden) and damage that only shows up after acceptance — because that is exactly where trade claims happen. *Spot it:* every firm working on other people's property has this exposure from job one; ask what happens on callbacks. *Claim example:* A plumber's solder joint fails six weeks after handover and floods the client's finished basement — around €35,000 in drying, flooring, and repainting. Without the working-damage and completed-work clauses, the policy could refuse exactly this. Verify the clause scope in the current conditions.
- **Motor/fleet cover (Kfz-/Flottenversicherung)** — motor liability is legally required for every vehicle; the comprehensive layer protects the firm's own vans and what a crash does to the business. *Spot it:* several vehicles means a fleet solution is worth checking; ask who drives and what rides along. *Claim example:* An apprentice rear-ends a car at a junction with the fully loaded transporter — €60,000 injury claim to the other driver plus a written-off van full of tools. The liability part is legally covered; whether the van and its schedule gap are covered depends on what was bound.
- **Contents + fixed-cost cover (Geschäftsinhalt + Betriebskosten/Betriebsunterbrechung)** — the contents policy replaces workshop, warehouse, and machinery at today's replacement value; the fixed-cost cover pays the rent, wages, and leasing that keep running while the business can't work. *Spot it:* any firm with a workshop or stored material; ask when the sum insured last saw an invoice. *Claim example:* A weekend burglary empties the workshop of €45,000 in machines and stock; the firm loses three weeks of jobs while replacing them, and €12,000 of fixed costs run on regardless. Two policies, one event.

**Recommended**
- **Bauleistungsversicherung (builders risk)** — covers the unfinished structure and materials already delivered but not yet built in. Neither the client's home policy nor the trade's own contents policy responds on a construction site. *Spot it:* new builds, shell construction, major renovations. *Claim example:* A storm tears the freshly erected roof truss off a shell build overnight — €55,000 in destroyed timber and re-erection. The building doesn't exist yet as far as any finished-building policy is concerned; only builders risk pays.
- **Montageversicherung (erection/installation)** — protects technical installations (heating systems, PV, electrical plant) during the installation phase, when they are delivered, part-assembled, and most vulnerable. *Spot it:* firms installing high-value systems rather than only building structures. *Claim example:* A delivered €18,000 heat pump is damaged by a dropped beam the day before commissioning. Not yet handed over, not yet the client's — the installation cover pays.
- **Kautionsversicherung (surety)** — not a claims policy but a guarantee product: the insurer issues warranty and performance bonds so clients don't withhold cash retentions and the bank credit line stays free. *Spot it:* clients withholding 5% retentions, public tenders, bond requirements in contracts. *How it plays out:* A firm has 5% retention withheld across eight running jobs — €40,000 of its own money locked up for years. Bonds release that cash back into the business the month they're issued.
- **Tools in transit** — an off-premises extension or transport cover for the tools and gear that live in the vans and on sites, where the building-based contents policy no longer reaches. *Spot it:* "my whole setup lives in the van," trailers loaded overnight. *Claim example:* A locked site trailer is broken open overnight and €22,000 of power tools disappear. The workshop contents policy denies it — the gear had left the premises. Verify overnight and vehicle clauses in the conditions.

**Situational**
- **Vermögensschaden-Haftpflicht (professional indemnity)** — covers pure financial loss from wrong planning and advice. The Betriebshaftpflicht covers the physical work; this covers the design judgment behind it. *Spot it:* the firm designs as well as builds — ask "do you plan, or only build to someone else's drawings?" *Claim example:* A design-and-build carpenter under-specifies a beam; the floor sags, and the owner sues over the planning error, not the workmanship — a €50,000 claim the liability policy would exclude as a planning fault.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz (legal expenses)** — pays lawyers and court costs when the firm has to fight: unpaid invoices, warranty accusations, employment disputes. *Spot it:* any firm that has ever "written off" an unpaid bill because suing felt too expensive. *Claim example:* A client refuses the final €28,000 invoice claiming defects; two years of expert reports and court run to €14,000 in legal costs before the firm wins. Rechtsschutz carried those costs — without it, many firms simply give up the claim.
- **Gruppen-Unfall, bKV, bAV (employee benefits)** — group accident cover pays where the statutory accident insurance doesn't: leisure time, lump sums for disability, the owner personally. bKV (company health) and bAV (company pension) are retention tools in a trade starved of skilled workers. *Spot it:* "we can't find people," or an owner with no personal accident cover at all. *Claim example:* A roofer shatters his ankle playing Sunday football — three months out, zero from the Berufsgenossenschaft because it wasn't a workplace accident. The group accident policy pays a lump sum and daily benefit, and the firm keeps a loyal employee instead of losing him to money stress.
- **Cyberversicherung** — pays for IT-incident response, data restoration, business interruption, and the fallout of payment fraud. Trade firms think they have nothing to hack; attackers know they have weak email security and big invoices. *Spot it:* digital quoting and invoicing, online banking. *Claim example:* The firm's email account is quietly hijacked; the fraudster sends the builder's real €19,000 final invoice with a swapped IBAN. The client pays the criminal, refuses to pay twice, and the relationship and the money are both gone. Cyber cover funds the forensics and, depending on the wording, the fraud loss — verify the payment-fraud module.

## 2. Cleaning & Facility Services
**Typical businesses:** Contract cleaning, office & practice cleaning, glass & facade cleaning, specialty cleaning, caretaker/janitorial services, post-construction cleaning
**Opening question:** "You work with master keys inside other people's buildings — what happens when a key goes missing or something breaks under your cloth?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — pays when the cleaning work injures someone or damages client property. For cleaners the whole risk lives inside other people's buildings, so the two clauses that matter are damage to the property being worked on (the desk you polish, the floor you strip) and key loss (Schlüsselverlust) — both restricted or capped in basic policies. *Spot it:* master keys, alarm codes, after-hours access. *Claim example:* A crew member loses the master key to an office complex; the landlord orders the entire locking system replaced — €25,000. A second classic: a visitor slips on a freshly mopped, unsigned lobby floor and fractures a hip — €60,000 in injury claims. Verify key-loss sublimits in the conditions.
- **Motor/fleet cover** — motor liability is legally required per vehicle, and cleaning crews are on the road between objects every day, which makes vehicle claims a frequency risk, not a rarity. *Spot it:* multiple objects serviced per day, crews in company cars. *Claim example:* Between the second and third building of the morning, a crew van clips a parked car in a narrow courtyard — €7,000. Small, but the third such claim in a year is a premium conversation; a fleet contract manages that better than five single policies.

**Recommended**
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz (legal expenses)** — pays the lawyer and court when the firm is dragged into a dispute. Cleaning is headcount-heavy with high turnover, so employment law is where the fights actually happen. *Spot it:* many employees, wage-hour questions, dismissal stories. *Claim example:* A dismissed cleaner files an unfair-dismissal suit; the labour court process costs €8,000 in legal fees even though the firm settles reasonably. In Germany each side pays its own lawyer at the labour court's first instance regardless of who wins — which is exactly why this cover earns its premium here.
- **Contents + fixed-cost cover** — replaces the machine park and stored supplies at replacement value and carries the fixed costs if the depot burns or floods. Growing cleaning firms accumulate more machinery than they realize. *Spot it:* ride-on scrubbers, storage units, a depot. *Claim example:* A break-in at the depot takes two ride-on scrubbing machines and a stock of chemicals — €18,000. The sum insured hadn't been touched since the firm had half the machines; without a buffer clause, underinsurance would have cut the payout.
- **Gruppen-Unfallversicherung (group accident)** — pays lump sums and daily benefits for accidents the statutory cover ignores, above all anything outside work. Facade and glass crews work at height, and their private lives carry the same body. *Spot it:* work at height, physically demanding crews. *Claim example:* A facade cleaner breaks a leg on a weekend hike — months off, and the Berufsgenossenschaft owes nothing because it wasn't a work accident. The group accident policy pays, and the employer who arranged it is remembered for it.

**Situational**
- **Cyberversicherung** — pays for incident response and liability when the firm's systems leak or lock. A cleaning company's scheduling software holds something uniquely sensitive: access codes and floor plans of client buildings. *Spot it:* scheduling apps, stored alarm codes, client portals. *Claim example:* The scheduling tool is breached and client alarm codes are exposed; every affected client must be notified, locks and codes changed, and a forensics firm engaged — €20,000 before any lawsuit, plus a trust problem only fast, funded response can contain.
- **bKV (company health insurance)** — a small employer-paid health top-up (dental, physio, screenings) that punches far above its cost in a low-wage, high-turnover sector. *Spot it:* complaints about churn and no-shows in recruiting. *How it plays out:* A cleaner gets a €900 dental bill largely covered by the employer's bKV — the kind of concrete, personal benefit that keeps someone from leaving for 50 cents more per hour elsewhere.

## 3. Landscaping & Grounds (GaLaBau, incl. winter service)
**Typical businesses:** Landscape construction, grounds maintenance, tree care, paving, winter/gritting service, irrigation
**Opening question:** "Who carries the gritting-duty liability when someone falls on a lot you maintain?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — pays when the work injures people or damages property, and in this trade two extensions decide everything: contractually assumed traffic-safety and gritting duties (the winter-service liability the property owner hands over), and the environmental modules for pesticide and fuel use. *Spot it:* winter contracts, spraying programs, work near neighboring property. *Claim example:* On a January morning, a pedestrian slips on black ice in a customer's parking lot the firm was contracted to grit — a fractured femur and a €45,000 injury claim aimed at the firm, not the owner, because the duty was contractually transferred. Verify winter service is expressly covered.
- **Mobile machinery cover (fahrbare Maschinen)** — a machinery policy for the self-propelled fleet: excavators, loaders, ride-on mowers. It covers theft, operating accidents, and damage wherever the machine works — which the building-based contents policy cannot. *Spot it:* machines parked on open sites or trailers overnight. *Claim example:* A €38,000 mini excavator vanishes from a fenced but unguarded site over a long weekend. No contents policy responds off-premises; the mobile machinery cover replaces it and the season is saved.
- **Fleet cover** — legally required liability per vehicle, plus the practical reality that this trade tows heavy trailers daily, and trailer incidents are its signature motor claim. *Spot it:* trucks with trailers, machines in tow. *Claim example:* A trailer loaded with a ride-on mower detaches on a federal road and hits the crash barrier and a following car — a liability claim in the tens of thousands plus the destroyed mower. The liability side is mandatory cover; the mower needs the machinery policy above.

**Recommended**
- **Bauleistungsversicherung (builders risk)** — covers the unfinished outdoor works: walls, paving, plantings, and delivered materials before handover, which no finished-property policy touches. *Spot it:* new installations, staged projects, tender work. *Claim example:* Days before handover, heavy rain undermines a newly built retaining wall and it partially collapses — €30,000 to rebuild. The client hasn't accepted the work; the loss is the contractor's, and builders risk is the policy that carries it.
- **Contents + fixed-cost cover** — replacement value for the hall, workshop, and the seasonal gear stored in it, plus the fixed costs that keep running when the base of operations is out of action. *Spot it:* a yard or hall full of equipment each winter. *Claim example:* A February hall fire destroys stored mowers, trimmers, and the irrigation stock for spring — €50,000 in equipment plus a scramble to be ready for the season while rent and wages run on.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — funds the legal fight when clients withhold payment claiming defects, the trade's most common dispute. *Spot it:* stories of unpaid final invoices, "the plants died" arguments. *Claim example:* A client withholds €22,000 claiming plantings failed; the expert report proves the client never watered as instructed. Getting there costs €9,000 in legal and expert fees — costs the legal-expenses policy carried.

**Situational**
- **Kautionsversicherung (surety)** — insurer-issued performance and warranty bonds, typically demanded on public tenders and larger commercial projects. *Spot it:* municipal contracts, bond clauses in tenders. *How it plays out:* A town requires a performance bond on a €200,000 park contract. The surety line issues it in days; without it, the bank would demand cash collateral and the firm couldn't bid.
- **Gruppen-Unfall / bKV** — accident lump sums beyond the statutory system and a health top-up for crews doing hard physical work. *Spot it:* physically worn teams, hiring trouble. *Claim example:* A landscaper cuts his hand badly with a chainsaw while cutting firewood at home — private, so the Berufsgenossenschaft pays nothing. The group accident policy pays the lump sum that bridges months of reduced capacity.
- **Cyberversicherung** — response and fraud cover once quoting, invoicing, and banking run digitally. *Spot it:* digital offers, email invoices. *Claim example:* A spoofed supplier email redirects a €15,000 materials payment to a fraudster's account. The money is gone before anyone calls; the cyber policy's fraud module and response team are the difference between a bad week and a bad year — verify the module is included.

## 4. Food Service & Hospitality
**Typical businesses:** Restaurants, cafés, fast food & franchise units, bars, hotels & guesthouses, catering
**Opening question:** "If the health authority closes your business tomorrow — how long can you carry the fixed costs?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — pays when guests are injured or made ill: the slip on a wet floor, the burn from a dropped plate, the food that sends a table to hospital, the coat that vanishes from the cloakroom. Alcohol service belongs in the wording as a matter of course. *Spot it:* every operation with guests on the premises — the question is only whether the wording fits the concept (bar, terrace, catering off-site). *Claim example:* A guest slips on a spilled drink nobody cleared, breaking a tooth and cheekbone — €25,000 including dental reconstruction. Second classic: a catering job leaves twelve wedding guests with salmonella, and the claims arrive as a group.
- **Geschäftsinhaltsversicherung (contents)** — replaces kitchen, refrigeration, and interior at today's replacement value after fire, water, burglary, or storm. Hospitality reinvests constantly, so the buffer (Vorsorge) and waiver of underinsurance are real money, not fine print. *Spot it:* any recent renovation or new kitchen line the sum insured has never heard of. *Claim example:* A fryer fire at closing time destroys the kitchen block — €80,000 in equipment. The policy was updated after the refit, so replacement value pays in full; the competitor down the street with a ten-year-old sum insured would have eaten a proportional cut.
- **Fixed-cost / business-interruption cover** — pays the rent, payroll, and leasing that continue while the restaurant can't serve. In a business of thin margins and high fixed costs, the interruption is often worse than the fire. *Spot it:* high-rent locations, financed fit-outs. *Claim example:* The kitchen fire above closes the restaurant for eight weeks. €50,000 of rent, wages, and leasing fall due with zero revenue — the interruption cover pays it, and the business reopens instead of folding.
- **Betriebsschließungsversicherung (ordered-closure cover)** — pays when the health authority closes the operation under the Infection Protection Act: salmonella, norovirus, a positive finding in the kitchen. This is the hospitality-specific policy most owners only learned about the hard way. *Spot it:* every food business; ask what a ten-day forced closure would cost. *Claim example:* A norovirus case traced to the kitchen triggers a ten-day ordered closure, deep disinfection, and destruction of open stock — roughly €30,000 in lost margin, disposal, and cleaning. Terms and covered diseases vary sharply by tariff generation — verify the current list.

**Recommended**
- **Commercial building cover (gewerbliche Gebäudeversicherung)** — fire, water, storm, and (ask actively) natural-hazards cover for owners of the property itself. *Spot it:* the operator owns the building or the family does. *Claim example:* A summer storm strips part of the roof over the guest rooms; €60,000 in building damage is the owner's problem, not the tenant's — and only a building policy responds.
- **Cyberversicherung** — response, restoration, and liability when the digital side fails: the reservation system, the POS, the guest data. *Spot it:* online booking, card payments, guest Wi-Fi. *Claim example:* Ransomware locks the POS and reservation system on a Friday; the weekend runs on paper and guesswork, online bookings bounce, and the recovery specialist costs €18,000. Guest-data exposure adds GDPR notification on top.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the two disputes hospitality actually has: the landlord and the staff. *Spot it:* rent negotiations, high staff turnover. *Claim example:* The landlord pushes a contested rent increase on the back of an index clause; fighting it through costs €10,000 in legal fees over 18 months — carried by the legal-expenses policy, which turned a "we can't afford to argue" into a negotiation.

**Situational**
- **bKV (company health insurance)** — an employer-paid health top-up that visibly cares for people in a sector where everyone is hiring. *Spot it:* "we're closed Mondays because we have no staff." *How it plays out:* The sous-chef gets fast-track physio for a shoulder through the bKV instead of a three-month wait — and turns down the offer from the hotel across town.
- **Gruppen-Unfall (group accident)** — lump sums for the burns, cuts, and falls of kitchen life, and for everything that happens off the clock where the statutory cover is silent. *Spot it:* kitchen-heavy teams. *Claim example:* A waiter scalds his hand badly at home canning fruit — no statutory cover, three weeks off. The group accident policy pays the daily benefit that keeps him whole.
- **Kunstversicherung (fine art)** — all-risk cover for art in hotels and upscale venues, which contents policies handle badly or cap low. *Spot it:* originals in the lobby, a collection in the restaurant. *Claim example:* A water leak ruins a €15,000 painting in the hotel lobby; the art policy pays at agreed value, where the contents policy would have argued about depreciation.

## 5. Retail
**Typical businesses:** Fashion & shoes, food & delicatessen, electronics & telecom, furniture & interiors, sports & leisure, kiosk & convenience
**Opening question:** "When did you last check your sum insured against the actual stock value on the floor — including the seasonal peak?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — pays when a customer is hurt in the shop or by the shop: the slip at the wet entrance, the shelf display that topples, the traffic-safety duty on the pavement outside. With public foot traffic all day, this is a frequency exposure. *Spot it:* every shop with a door that opens to the public. *Claim example:* On a rainy Saturday a customer slips just inside the entrance — no mat, no sign — and breaks a wrist. €15,000 in injury claims and a lesson about the doormat that the policy absorbed.
- **Geschäftsinhaltsversicherung (contents)** — replaces stock, fittings, and equipment at replacement value after fire, burglary, water, or storm. Stock *is* the working capital, and it swings with the season, which is why the buffer and the waiver of underinsurance decide whether a payout is full or cut. *Spot it:* ask what's on the floor and in the back at the seasonal peak versus what's in the policy. *Claim example:* A burglary in late November clears €70,000 of Christmas stock. The policy's sum insured reflected a quiet March; only the buffer clause and waiver saved the payout from a proportional reduction that would have cost the owner tens of thousands.
- **Fixed-cost / business-interruption cover** — pays the prime-location rent and wages while the shop is closed for repairs. In retail the rent doesn't care that the shop is full of dehumidifiers. *Spot it:* A-location rents, financed fit-outs. *Claim example:* A burst pipe above the ceiling closes the shop for six weeks in its best quarter. €25,000 of rent and payroll fall due against zero revenue — the interruption cover pays, and the missed season doesn't become a missed year.

**Recommended**
- **Cyberversicherung** — response, restoration, and liability when the webshop, the POS, or the customer database is hit. The moment a shop sells or markets online, it holds exactly what attackers monetize: payment flows and customer data. *Spot it:* a webshop, newsletter lists, card terminals. *Claim example:* Card-skimming code sits in the webshop checkout for three weeks; forensics, mandatory customer notification, and platform cleanup cost €25,000 — before a cent of the reputational repair.
- **Elektronikversicherung** — an all-risk policy for the electronics the shop runs on: tills, scanners, label printers, digital signage. It pays for the surge, the drop, and the spilled coffee that a standard contents policy calls "operating damage" and declines. *Spot it:* modern POS setups, screens in the window. *Claim example:* A power surge after grid works kills the POS system and two display screens — €12,000, paid as an electronics claim where the contents policy would have argued.
- **Transport cover (goods)** — insures the shop's goods while they move: from the wholesaler, between branches, in the owner's own van. The contents policy stops at the shop door. *Spot it:* own pickup runs, multi-branch stock transfers. *Claim example:* The owner's van is rear-ended on a stock run between branches; €18,000 of fashion stock is crushed and soaked. Motor liability pays the other driver — only transport cover pays for the goods.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for retail's two chronic disputes: the commercial lease and employment. *Spot it:* index-rent letters, staffing churn. *Claim example:* The landlord invokes an index clause for a steep rent increase; contesting the calculation takes €8,000 in legal fees and ends in a compromise the shop could live with — a fight it could only afford because the policy paid the lawyers.

**Situational**
- **Betriebsschließungsversicherung (ordered-closure cover)** — pays when the health authority closes a food retailer under the Infection Protection Act. *Spot it:* any shop selling unpackaged food. *Claim example:* A rodent finding closes a delicatessen for a week of pest control and deep cleaning — €12,000 in lost margin and destroyed open goods, plus the cleanup bill.
- **Commercial building cover** — for the retailer who owns the building: fire, water, storm, and the natural-hazards question asked out loud. *Spot it:* owner-occupied premises. *Claim example:* Storm damage to the facade and roof runs to €40,000 — a building-policy claim that neither contents nor interruption cover touches.
- **Gruppen-Unfall / bKV (benefits)** — accident lump sums beyond the statutory system and a health top-up, as retention tools in branch operations that constantly hire. *Spot it:* multiple branches, chronic vacancies. *How it plays out:* A branch manager who gets employer-paid dental and a private accident lump sum after a cycling crash tells every new hire about it — cheaper than one recruiter invoice.

## 6. Real Estate & Property Management
**Typical businesses:** Property managers (WEG & rental), portfolio landlords, estate agents, small-scale development, facility owners, holiday letting
**Opening question:** "You manage other people's money — is the liability for that insured the way professional law requires?"

**Must-have**
- **Vermögensschaden-Haftpflichtversicherung (professional indemnity)** — pays when a professional mistake costs clients money without anything being physically damaged: a missed deadline, a wrong statement, a botched owners' resolution. For residential property managers and estate agents this cover is required by professional law — and pure financial loss is precisely what the Betriebshaftpflicht excludes. *Spot it:* every licensed manager and agent; verify the level against the current statutory minimums. *Claim example:* A manager lets a WEG building's insurance lapse during a carrier switch; a fire then finds the building underinsured, and the owners sue the manager for the €100,000+ difference. That claim lands on the PI policy — or on the manager personally.
- **Betriebshaftpflicht incl. property-owner liability (Haus- und Grundbesitzerhaftpflicht)** — pays when someone is injured because of the building or the ground: the icy pavement, the loose step, the falling tile. Managers carry it for managed objects, owners for their own. *Spot it:* every managed or owned building. *Claim example:* A loose roof tile comes down in a gust and injures a pedestrian — a €50,000 bodily-injury claim against the owner for breached maintenance duty, defended and paid by the property-owner liability cover.
- **Commercial building cover** — the core asset protection for portfolio holders: fire, water, storm — and the natural-hazards (Elementar) module raised proactively, because heavy-rain losses are the fastest-growing claim in the segment. *Spot it:* every owned building; ask which ones have Elementar. *Claim example:* A cloudburst backs up the sewer and floods a building's basement and heating plant — €70,000. Without the Elementar module, the standard building policy pays nothing on backflow and surface water.

**Recommended**
- **Mietkautionsversicherung (rental deposit insurance)** — replaces the cash deposit with a guarantee, so tenants don't tie up three months' rent and landlords still hold security. *Spot it:* every landlord conversation about new lettings. *How it plays out:* A commercial tenant moves in without parking €9,000 cash; the landlord holds an insurer's guarantee instead and lets the unit faster — a product that sells itself in the letting conversation.
- **Photovoltaik cover** — insures the rooftop plant itself (storm, hail, fire, technical failure, theft) and the feed-in revenue it earns. Building policies don't automatically pick up a PV plant cleanly. *Spot it:* PV going onto portfolio roofs, WEG resolutions on solar. *Claim example:* An inverter fault starts a small roof fire; €35,000 in panel, roof, and revenue loss. The building insurer points at the PV plant, the PV insurer at the roof — unless the allocation was sorted at placement, which is the agent's job.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the disputes that fill a manager's week: rent arrears, evictions, owner-association conflicts. *Spot it:* any manager with open cases. *Claim example:* An eviction for arrears grinds through 18 months of court — €12,000 in legal costs before the unit is recovered. The legal-expenses policy carried it; the rent shortfall was bad enough.
- **Bauleistungsversicherung (builders risk)** — covers refurbishment and roof projects in the portfolio while they're half-done, when neither the building policy nor the contractor's cover fully protects the owner's interest. *Spot it:* planned facade, roof, or energy refits. *Claim example:* Mid-refurbishment, a storm drives rain through the opened roof into three renovated floors — €45,000. The building policy calls it a construction risk; builders risk is the policy built for exactly this window.

**Situational**
- **Cyberversicherung** — response and liability when the manager's systems are breached. A management firm holds bank details, service-charge accounts, and personal data for entire owner communities — a rich, soft target. *Spot it:* digital owner portals, SEPA collection runs. *Claim example:* The manager's email is compromised and owners receive a convincing "new account for service-charge payments" — €30,000 lands with a fraudster across a dozen owners before anyone calls. Response costs, notification, and liability follow.
- **Kunstversicherung** — agreed-value cover for art in representative lobbies and managed prestige objects. *Spot it:* originals in entrance halls. *Claim example:* Movers damage a lobby sculpture during a refit — €10,000 at agreed value, no depreciation argument.

## 7. Professional & Financial Services
**Typical businesses:** Management consulting, financial & insurance intermediaries, payroll & bookkeeping services, surveyors & experts, recruiting, translation
**Opening question:** "Your product is your advice — what happens when a piece of advice was demonstrably wrong and the client puts a number on the damage?"

**Must-have**
- **Vermögensschaden-Haftpflichtversicherung (professional indemnity)** — pays when advice, a calculation, or a missed deadline costs the client money. This is the segment's core policy because the product *is* the advice, and errors here never break anything physical — they only destroy value, which is exactly what general liability excludes. For several licensed professions it is legally mandatory. *Spot it:* every advisory business; check mandatory status and minimums per license. *Claim example:* A payroll service applies the wrong social-contribution parameters for a year; the client faces €60,000 in back payments and penalties and passes the bill straight to the provider. PI defends, negotiates, and pays what's owed.
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — the ordinary premises-and-people liability: a visitor trips, a laptop bag knocks a client's equipment off the desk. Small next to the PI risk, but the claims are real and cheap to cover. *Spot it:* any office with visitors or work at client sites. *Claim example:* A client's representative trips over a presentation cable in the meeting room and fractures an elbow — €12,000, cleanly a general-liability claim, cleanly excluded from PI.
- **Cyberversicherung** — response, restoration, notification, and liability when client data leaks or systems lock. For an advisory firm the data *is* the relationship: mandates, financials, personal records. *Spot it:* cloud tools, remote work, client portals. *Claim example:* Ransomware encrypts the file server including years of client records; recovery, forensics, and GDPR notification to every affected client cost €30,000 — and the funded, professional response is what keeps the top three mandates from leaving.

**Recommended**
- **Contents + Elektronikversicherung** — replacement-value cover for the office and an all-risk layer for the hardware it actually runs on: laptops, servers, presentation tech — including drops, surges, and theft from the car, which contents policies exclude or cap. *Spot it:* mobile teams, financed IT. *Claim example:* A burst pipe over the weekend soaks the server cabinet and four workstations — €22,000, split between the contents claim (furniture, fit-out) and the electronics claim (the hardware, at conditions a plain contents policy wouldn't match).
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the firm's own fights, above all unpaid fees and employment disputes — the two cases advisory firms swallow most often because suing a client feels expensive. *Spot it:* receivables complaints, growing headcount. *Claim example:* A client refuses a €25,000 project fee claiming the deliverable "wasn't usable"; two instances of litigation cost €11,000 in fees — paid by the policy, and the firm collected rather than writing it off.
- **Dienstreiseversicherung (business travel)** — medical, evacuation, luggage, and liability cover for staff traveling on business, where statutory and private health cover leave gaps abroad. *Spot it:* consultants at client sites, international projects. *Claim example:* A consultant collapses with appendicitis at a client site abroad; treatment and medically escorted return run to €15,000 — a travel-policy claim the firm would otherwise have paid from goodwill.

**Situational**
- **bAV / bKV (pension & health benefits)** — employer-funded pension and health top-ups, the standard currency in the competition for qualified staff. *Spot it:* hiring frustration, counteroffers. *How it plays out:* A senior analyst weighing two offers takes the one with a funded bAV and employer bKV — benefits that cost the firm a fraction of the salary bump the competitor would have needed.
- **Gruppen-Unfallversicherung (group accident)** — lump-sum accident cover reaching where the statutory system doesn't: leisure time, and the owner, who often has no statutory accident cover at all. *Spot it:* owner-managers with no personal accident policy. *Claim example:* The founding partner tears knee ligaments skiing — three months of reduced capacity, zero statutory benefits because it was private and she's the owner. The group accident policy's lump sum bridges what her absence costs.

## 8. IT & Digital Services
**Typical businesses:** Software development, IT system houses & managed services, web development, IT consulting, hosting & cloud, e-commerce services
**Opening question:** "If your update stops the client's production line — who carries the downtime?"

**Must-have**
- **Vermögensschaden-Haftpflicht for IT (IT professional indemnity)** — pays when the firm's code, configuration, or advice costs the client money without breaking anything physical: the botched update, the misconfigured firewall, the data migration that loses records. This is the IT firm's core liability, because its errors are almost never property damage. *Spot it:* every firm shipping software or managing client systems; check the IT wording and how it combines with general liability. *Claim example:* A faulty release halts a client's production planning for two days; the client invoices €120,000 in standstill costs. General liability shrugs — nothing was damaged. The IT-PI policy defends and pays.
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — the physical-world liability that still exists in a digital business: the technician who drops a client's switch, the visitor who trips in the office. Usually bundled with the IT-PI, and the bundle is the point. *Spot it:* on-site work at client premises. *Claim example:* During a rack migration, a technician's slip sends a client's storage unit to the floor — €18,000 of hardware. Physical damage, so this is the general-liability half of the package paying.
- **Cyberversicherung (the firm's own)** — response, restoration, interruption, and liability when the IT firm itself is breached. Doubly critical here: the firm's downtime cascades into every client it manages, and an IT provider that can't fund its own professional incident response loses the room. *Spot it:* admin access to client systems, managed-services contracts. *Claim example:* An attacker compromises the MSP's remote-management tool and pivots toward client networks. Containment, forensics across environments, and client notification cost €80,000 — and the insurer's response team working alongside the firm is what keeps clients from leaving.

**Recommended**
- **Elektronikversicherung** — all-risk cover for the development and server hardware, including surge, drop, and theft — the failure modes contents policies exclude. *Spot it:* own rack, high-spec workstations. *Claim example:* A failed UPS lets a surge through and cooks two servers and a switch — €16,000, paid as an electronics claim.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the sector's signature dispute: the client who won't sign off. Project acceptance, scope, and payment fights are where IT firms bleed quietly. *Spot it:* "the client won't accept the release." *Claim example:* A client refuses acceptance of a €60,000 platform build citing "missing features" that were never in scope; the legal battle to enforce payment costs €14,000 in fees — carried by the policy, and the invoice gets paid.
- **Contents + fixed-cost cover** — replacement value for the office and the fixed costs that run if it's unusable. Lean, but real once premises and people exist. *Spot it:* own office, growing team. *Claim example:* An electrical fire makes the office unusable for a month; €10,000 of rent and recurring costs run against a team working from kitchens — the fixed-cost cover pays while the lease doesn't care.

**Situational**
- **bAV (company pension)** — employer-funded pension, near hygiene-factor in IT recruiting. *Spot it:* every hiring conversation in this sector. *How it plays out:* A developer choosing between offers asks about the pension scheme in the first interview — the firm with a funded bAV answers in one sentence; the firm without loses candidates it never hears about.
- **Dienstreiseversicherung (business travel)** — medical and evacuation cover for project staff at client sites. *Spot it:* consultants embedded at customers, international rollouts. *Claim example:* An engineer on a rollout abroad lands in hospital after a scooter accident; treatment and return transport cost €12,000 — a travel-policy claim, not a payroll dispute.

## 9. Agencies, Marketing & Media
**Typical businesses:** Advertising & creative agencies, social media & performance marketing, photo & video production, PR & communications, design & branding, publishing & content
**Opening question:** "Who is liable when a campaign infringes an image, trademark, or personality right — you or your client?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht + Vermögensschaden-Haftpflicht (with media wording)** — the pair that covers agency life: general liability for the physical world (the set, the office, the event), and PI for the real agency risk — published work that infringes someone's rights or a campaign error that costs the client money. Rights infringement and flawed execution are pure financial losses, and only a media-aware PI wording carries them. *Spot it:* every firm that publishes for clients; verify which module carries rights infringement before quoting. *Claim example:* A campaign ships with a stock photo whose license didn't cover outdoor advertising; the photographer's agency claims €20,000. Second classic: a booking error runs the client's ad in the wrong market, and the client invoices the wasted €45,000 media spend.
- **Cyberversicherung** — response and liability when the agency's accounts or systems are hit. Agencies hold something uniquely dangerous: admin access to clients' ad accounts, shops, and social channels, plus budget authority over other people's money. *Spot it:* managed ad accounts, client logins in a password tool. *Claim example:* A phished login lets an attacker into a client's ad account overnight; €35,000 of the client's budget is burned on fraud placements before the alarm. The client wants it back from the agency — cyber cover funds the response and the liability discussion.

**Recommended**
- **Elektronikversicherung** — all-risk cover for camera, edit, and production gear, including the failure modes that matter on location: drop, theft from the vehicle, water. *Spot it:* shoots, kits that travel. *Claim example:* A camera kit worth €28,000 is stolen from the crew van during a location lunch — an electronics/equipment claim; the office contents policy stopped applying at the office door.
- **Contents + fixed-cost cover** — replacement value for studio and fit-out plus the fixed costs that run if the space is out of action. *Spot it:* own studio, edit suites. *Claim example:* A sprinkler fault soaks the studio and edit suite — €30,000 in fit-out and idle weeks of rent, split between the contents and fixed-cost claims.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the agency's chronic fights: fees and usage rights after the relationship sours. *Spot it:* scope-creep stories, clients using work beyond the license. *Claim example:* A former client keeps running campaign assets two years after the license ended; enforcing the usage rights costs €9,000 in legal fees — carried by the policy, and the settlement paid for the year.

**Situational**
- **Dienstreiseversicherung (business travel)** — medical, evacuation, and equipment-adjacent cover for productions and pitches away from base. *Spot it:* shoots abroad, traveling crews. *Claim example:* A producer breaks an ankle on a foreign location; treatment and early return cost €8,000 — a travel-policy claim, not a production overrun.
- **Kunstversicherung** — agreed-value cover for agencies with collections or exhibition space. *Spot it:* originals on the walls, gallery-style offices. *Claim example:* A client event leaves a €12,000 piece damaged; the art policy pays at agreed value while a contents policy would have argued depreciation.

## 10. Healthcare & Care Services
**Typical businesses:** Physiotherapy, occupational & speech therapy, alternative practitioners (Heilpraktiker), outpatient care services, midwife practices, podiatry
**Opening question:** "A treatment-error allegation eventually hits every practice — who runs your defense and pays a justified claim?"

**Must-have**
- **Business & professional liability (Betriebs-/Berufshaftpflicht)** — pays when treatment injures a patient, and defends when a patient merely alleges it did. In hands-on professions, bodily injury from treatment is the core risk, professional law usually requires the cover, and the defense function matters as much as the payout: the unfounded allegation still needs experts and lawyers. *Spot it:* every treating practice; verify the professional-law requirement per profession. *Claim example:* A physiotherapy manipulation is blamed for worsening a disc injury; the patient claims €70,000. The expert battle takes two years — the policy funds the defense throughout and pays only what's actually owed.
- **Contents + Elektronikversicherung** — replacement value for the practice fit-out plus all-risk cover for the treatment devices, which are financed, sensitive, and worth more than anyone remembers. *Spot it:* device-heavy practices, leasing contracts. *Claim example:* A weekend water leak ruins the shockwave unit and two treatment tables — €25,000, with the device paid under the electronics cover at terms a plain contents policy wouldn't offer.
- **Fixed-cost cover (Betriebskosten)** — pays the rent and salaries that continue while the practice can't treat or bill. Practices bill per session; when the rooms close, revenue is zero on day one. *Spot it:* high fit-out financing, salaried therapists. *Claim example:* Water damage closes the practice for five weeks; €30,000 of rent, salaries, and leasing fall due against no billable sessions — the fixed-cost cover pays and the practice reopens solvent.

**Recommended**
- **Cyberversicherung** — response, restoration, and notification when patient data is exposed or the practice software locks. Patient records are health data — the most sensitive GDPR category, with the strictest duties and the ugliest headlines. *Spot it:* digital patient files, online booking. *Claim example:* Ransomware encrypts the practice software on a Monday: no schedules, no records, no billing. Recovery and mandatory notification cost €22,000 — and the funded incident response is what keeps a data-protection complaint from becoming a fine.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the disputes practices actually face: billing clawbacks from payers, employment conflicts, professional-law proceedings. Sector-specific healthcare variants exist — check availability. *Spot it:* payer audits, growing staff. *Claim example:* A health-insurer audit demands €18,000 in billing clawbacks; contesting it with specialized counsel costs €7,000 and cuts the clawback by two-thirds — a fight worth having only because the policy paid for it.
- **Fleet cover** — for outpatient care services, the cars *are* the operation: a dozen small vehicles on daily tours mean motor claims are a monthly reality, and one uninsured gap stops tours. *Spot it:* care services with route plans. *Claim example:* A carer's morning tour ends in a junction collision — injury liability plus a written-off car. The fleet contract absorbs both and swaps in a replacement vehicle before the afternoon tour.

**Situational**
- **Gruppen-Unfall / bKV (benefits)** — accident lump sums beyond the statutory system and an employer health top-up — which is doubly credible coming from a health-sector employer. *Spot it:* physically loaded teams, recruiting pain. *Claim example:* A carer herniates a disc lifting at home — private, so no statutory accident benefits. The group accident policy pays a lump sum, and the employer's bKV fast-tracks her physio; both stories travel through the team.
- **Betriebsschließungsversicherung (ordered-closure cover)** — pays when the health authority closes a facility under the Infection Protection Act. *Spot it:* day-care structures, group settings. *Claim example:* A norovirus outbreak closes a day-care facility for a week of disinfection — lost fees and cleaning costs around €10,000, covered where the wording includes the facility type; verify scope.

## 11. Personal Care & Fitness
**Typical businesses:** Hair salons, cosmetics & nail studios, barbershops, gyms, yoga & personal training, non-therapeutic massage
**Opening question:** "What happens when a treatment or a training session causes a health injury to the client?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — pays when the service itself injures the client — the chemical burn, the allergic reaction, the training injury from faulty instruction — and when the premises do it: the wet floor, the loose mat. In this segment the "treatment damage" clause is the heart of the policy, because the injury happens *through* the service. *Spot it:* every hands-on or instruction-based business; ask about new services (peels, EMS) the wording may not know. *Claim example:* A coloring goes wrong and the client suffers scalp burns and patchy hair loss — €18,000 in damages and treatment costs. In the gym version, a trainer's misloaded machine drops a weight on a member's foot.
- **Contents + fixed-cost cover** — replacement value for the fit-out and equipment plus the rent and wages that continue while the studio is closed. These businesses are their rooms; no rooms, no revenue. *Spot it:* fresh renovations, leased equipment. *Claim example:* A night fire guts a salon a week after its refit — €40,000 in fit-out and stock, plus six weeks of rent and wages carried by the fixed-cost cover while the rebuild runs.

**Recommended**
- **Cyberversicherung** — response and notification when booking systems and member data are exposed. Studios hold names, health notes, and payment mandates — a full identity kit. *Spot it:* booking apps, subscription billing. *Claim example:* The booking platform account is breached and member payment data exposed; notification, forensics, and platform migration cost €12,000 — and the calm, funded response is what keeps members from cancelling.

- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the lease and the team, the two disputes this segment actually has. *Spot it:* rent negotiations, trainer churn. *Claim example:* The landlord contests the permitted use when the salon adds cosmetic treatments; sorting the lease dispute costs €6,000 in fees — carried by the policy.

**Situational**
- **bKV / Gruppen-Unfall (benefits)** — health top-up and accident lump sums as retention tools in a high-churn trade where trained staff can open next door. *Spot it:* churn complaints. *How it plays out:* A stylist stays through a poaching attempt because the employer's bKV covered her €800 dental bill last spring — loyalty bought at a fraction of a rehire.
- **Commercial building cover** — for the owner-occupied studio: fire, water, storm on the building itself. *Spot it:* owned premises. *Claim example:* Storm damage to the studio's roof and glazing runs €20,000 — a building claim neither contents nor interruption cover touches.

## 12. Automotive Trades
**Typical businesses:** Independent workshops, body & paint, tire service, detailing, car dealers, towing & roadside
**Opening question:** "Customer vehicles sit on your lot every night — whose insurance pays when something happens there?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht with an automotive-trade wording** — pays for injury and damage from the operation, and — the existential part — for damage to customer vehicles in the firm's custody: moving them, working on them, storing them. A standard liability policy classically *excludes* property in custody, which in this trade is the entire business model. The trade solution also carries dealer plates and test drives. *Spot it:* every yard full of customer cars; verify the custody wording and red-plate cover before promising anything. *Claim example:* An employee moving a customer's car in the yard misjudges a pillar — €22,000 on a premium vehicle. On a standard policy: excluded as custody damage. On the trade wording: paid. Second classic: the test drive that ends in a junction.
- **Geschäftsinhaltsversicherung (contents)** — replacement value for the workshop's working capital: lifts, diagnostics, tools, and the parts inventory. *Spot it:* recently upgraded diagnostic and calibration equipment the sum insured predates. *Claim example:* A break-in targets exactly what resells: €30,000 of diagnostic units and tool trolleys gone in a night — replaced at today's prices because the sum insured was current.
- **Environmental modules** — the liability extensions for what a workshop necessarily handles: oils, fuels, coolants, batteries. When they escape, the bill is a public-law cleanup order, not a normal damages claim — and only the environmental modules respond. *Spot it:* every workshop; ask how fluids are stored and where the separator drains. *Claim example:* A failed oil separator lets waste oil into the soil; the authority orders excavation and remediation — €50,000, payable regardless of fault. Verify the environmental liability and environmental-damage scope in the conditions.

**Recommended**
- **Fixed-cost cover** — pays the rent, wages, and leasing that run while the workshop can't work. Bays full of half-finished jobs earn nothing during a rebuild. *Spot it:* leased premises, financed equipment. *Claim example:* An electrical fire closes the workshop for seven weeks; €35,000 of fixed costs fall due against zero throughput — paid by the interruption cover, and the customer base survives the closure because the firm does.
- **Maschinenversicherung (machinery breakdown)** — pays for the internal failure of high-value stationary equipment — the breakdown from within that fire and contents policies don't cover. *Spot it:* lifts, alignment rigs, paint booths. *Claim example:* A lift's hydraulic system fails mid-service — €15,000 in repair and the near-miss report that changes how everyone reads "machinery breakdown."
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the trade's permanent condition: disputed repair invoices and warranty accusations. *Spot it:* stories of customers refusing to pay. *Claim example:* A customer disputes a €4,000 engine repair and threatens court; the legal-expenses policy pays for the lawyer and the expert who proves the diagnosis — the invoice gets paid and the Google review war stays short.

**Situational**
- **Cyberversicherung** — response cover for the workshop's digital spine: planning software, diagnostic systems, customer data. *Spot it:* digital job cards, connected diagnostics. *Claim example:* Ransomware locks the workshop-management system; no job cards, no history, no invoicing for a week — €14,000 in recovery and lost throughput.
- **Gruppen-Unfall / bKV (benefits)** — accident lump sums and a health top-up for a trade competing hard for mechanics. *Spot it:* open positions measured in months. *Claim example:* A mechanic breaks a wrist mountain-biking — no statutory cover, six weeks out; the group accident policy pays the daily benefit that keeps him financially whole.
- **Kautionsversicherung (surety)** — insurer-issued bonds where franchise or partner contracts demand security. *Spot it:* brand-partner agreements with bond clauses. *How it plays out:* A service-partner contract requires a €25,000 guarantee; the surety line issues it without touching the credit line.

## 13. Education & Training
**Typical businesses:** Private training providers, tutoring institutes, driving schools, music & art schools, coaching & seminars, language schools
**Opening question:** "You carry a duty of supervision over other people's — often underage — children. Is it insured the way you're liable?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht** — pays when participants are injured on the premises, at events, or on excursions — and defends the supervision-duty allegation that follows every incident involving a minor. Parents' expectations are high and emotional; the policy's defense function matters as much as its payout. *Spot it:* minors in courses, off-site activities, group sizes. *Claim example:* On a course excursion a nine-year-old falls from a climbing frame and breaks an arm; the parents allege inadequate supervision and claim €40,000. The policy funds the defense and pays what the supervision facts actually support.

**Recommended**
- **Cyberversicherung** — response and notification when participant data or course platforms are hit. Education providers hold children's data and parents' payment details — a combination with zero public tolerance for sloppy handling. *Spot it:* online course platforms, installment billing. *Claim example:* The registration platform is breached and participant data including children's records exposed; forensics and notification to every family cost €15,000 — and the professionally handled response is what keeps enrollment from collapsing.

Relevant the moment outcomes are promised. *Spot it:* certified qualifications, exam-prep promises. *Claim example:* An exam-prep provider gives wrong registration-deadline advice and a student loses a year; the family quantifies tuition and lost income at €15,000 — a pure financial loss only PI covers.

**Situational**

Frequency is the story here; the dedicated driving-school wording prices and manages it.
- **Gruppen-Unfallversicherung (group accident)** — participant accident cover as a quality marker private providers can offer, since statutory pupil accident insurance covers school contexts, not private courses. *Spot it:* parents asking about insurance on the enrollment form. *Claim example:* A participant sprains an ankle in a dance course; the provider's group accident policy pays a benefit without any liability question — and the enrollment brochure that mentions this cover wins the next comparison against the studio that doesn't.
- **Dienstreiseversicherung (business travel)** — medical and evacuation cover for traveling instructors and trainers. *Spot it:* trainers on the road, seminars delivered abroad. *Claim example:* A trainer falls ill at a seminar abroad; treatment and rebooking cost €5,000 — a travel-policy claim.

## 14. Events & Entertainment
**Typical businesses:** Event agencies, event technology & rental, trade-fair construction, catering & mobile food, DJs & artist services, wedding & event planning
**Opening question:** "Your most expensive gear is least protected exactly when it earns money — in transit and on the floor. True for you too?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht with an organizer wording (Veranstalterhaftpflicht)** — pays when visitors are injured or property is damaged at the event: the structure that fails, the cable run someone trips on, the assumed traffic-safety duties of the whole venue. Standard business liability doesn't automatically carry organizer risk, and tariffs cap the event sizes and types they'll accept. *Spot it:* every firm running or building events; clarify covered sizes and formats in advance. *Claim example:* A truss element comes down during a show and seriously injures a visitor — a €150,000 bodily-injury claim against the organizer. This is the claim the organizer wording exists for, and the one a standard policy would fight about.
- **Elektronikversicherung + transport cover** — the pair that follows the gear: all-risk electronics cover for sound, light, and video (including rented and loaned units), and transport cover for the hours in the van — because the stationary contents policy ends at the warehouse door and event gear lives on the road. *Spot it:* loaded vans overnight before shows, rented kit in the mix. *Claim example:* The night before a festival, €60,000 of lighting rig is stolen from the van at a motorway hotel. Contents policy: not on premises, denied. The electronics/transport combination: paid, and a rental replacement funded in time for doors.
- **Fleet cover** — legally required liability per vehicle, and operationally the backbone: vans and trailers moving heavy, awkward loads on deadline. *Spot it:* every technology and build operation. *Claim example:* A trailer of stage decking jackknifes on a wet ramp, damaging the trailer, the load, and a parked car — liability, own-damage, and cargo questions in one incident, sorted cleanly only because fleet, transport, and equipment covers were placed to interlock.

**Recommended**
- **Montageversicherung (erection cover)** — protects stages, stands, and structures during build-up and tear-down, the phase where most physical losses happen and where the boundary to liability cover needs to be drawn deliberately. *Spot it:* own stage or trade-fair construction. *Claim example:* During build-up a stage section collapses, wrecking the section and gouging the venue's floor — €25,000 across own-structure damage (erection cover) and venue damage (liability). Knowing which policy takes which half is the placement work.
- **Contents cover** — replacement value for the warehouse and workshop where the gear winters. *Spot it:* off-season storage full of value. *Claim example:* A January warehouse fire takes staging, cabling, and casework — €45,000, at a moment when there's no revenue to absorb it.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the sector's constant fights: cancellations, contract disputes, unpaid fees. *Spot it:* cancellation stories, deposit arguments. *Claim example:* A corporate client cancels a produced event two weeks out and refuses the cancellation fee; enforcing the €30,000 contract costs €8,000 in legal fees — paid by the policy.

**Situational**
- **Cyberversicherung** — response and notification when ticketing systems and attendee data are hit. *Spot it:* own ticket shops, attendee databases. *Claim example:* The ticket shop is skimmed for a month; card-data notification and forensics cost €18,000 before the refund wave.
- **Gruppen-Unfall (group accident)** — lump sums for riggers and build crews whose risk profile doesn't end with the shift. *Spot it:* crews working at height. *Claim example:* A rigger is hurt climbing privately on a day off — no statutory cover; the group accident lump sum bridges the months of recovery.
- **Event cancellation (Veranstaltungsausfallversicherung)** — covers the costs and lost revenue when an event can't happen (weather, illness of key performers, venue failure). A specialty line with narrow appetite and event-by-event underwriting. *Spot it:* whenever a client's whole year hangs on one date. *How it plays out:* An open-air event is rained off and €80,000 in sunk costs evaporate — the loss cancellation cover exists for. Log the need and place it case by case; never improvise an answer on terms.

## 15. Light Manufacturing & Wholesale Distribution
**Typical businesses:** Metal & plastics processing, small food production, packaging & assembly, technical wholesale, building-materials trade, import & distribution
**Opening question:** "If your key machine stops tomorrow or a product has to be recalled — which of the two ruins you faster?"

**Must-have**
- **Betriebshaftpflicht / industrial liability with extended product liability** — pays when the firm's product injures someone or damages something after it leaves the gate. The moment goods are produced, processed, or sold under the firm's own name, the extended product-liability wording (erweiterte Produkthaftpflicht) is the core — it covers the expensive downstream scenarios like the customer's costs of removing and reinstalling a defective part. Recall costs (Rückrufkosten) need to be raised proactively, because a recall hits even the faultless supplier in the chain. Beyond a certain size, this becomes an industrial placement rather than a standard commercial tariff. *Spot it:* own-name products, private-label production, supplying into larger brands. *Claim example:* A machined component fails inside a customer's assembly; the customer recalls the batch and passes on €90,000 in removal, reinstallation, and recall logistics. Basic product liability would pay a fraction; the extended wording with recall cover carries it.
- **Contents + Maschinenversicherung (contents + machinery breakdown)** — replacement value for plant and warehouses, plus machinery-breakdown cover for the failure *from within* — the spindle crash, the drive failure — that fire policies don't touch. *Spot it:* production lines, CNC parks, full raw-material stores. *Claim example:* A CNC spindle crash wrecks the machining center — €45,000 in repair. No fire, no storm, no burglary: only the machinery policy responds, and the interruption cover (next) picks up the standstill.
- **Business interruption** — pays the lost contribution margin and continuing costs while production stands. In manufacturing the bottleneck is existential: one machine, one warehouse, one key customer. *Spot it:* ask which single machine's failure stops everything. *Claim example:* A fire on the single production line stops output for three months; €250,000 of contribution margin and running costs are the real loss — several times the fire damage itself. This is the cover that decides whether the customer list survives.

**Recommended**
- **Transport cover (goods / carrier liability)** — insures the firm's goods in motion, inbound and outbound, and the liability of carrying for others where relevant. In wholesale, goods in transit are the second inventory. *Spot it:* import containers, own delivery fleet, Incoterms talk. *Claim example:* An inbound container arrives seawater-damaged — €35,000 of stock ruined before it ever reached the warehouse the contents policy protects. Transport cover pays; the contents policy never could.
- **Fleet cover** — legally required per vehicle and operationally central for delivery operations. *Spot it:* own trucks on daily routes. *Claim example:* A delivery truck accident writes off the vehicle and injures another driver — the fleet contract absorbs the liability and own-damage sides and keeps the delivery schedule alive with a replacement.
- **Cyberversicherung** — response, restoration, and interruption cover for the production IT: ERP, warehouse management, machine controls. Ransomware downtime is the modern business interruption, and property policies don't cover it. *Spot it:* ERP-run planning, connected machines. *Claim example:* Ransomware encrypts the ERP and warehouse system; picking, production planning, and invoicing stop for ten days — €120,000 in interruption and recovery, none of it a property claim.

**Situational**
- **Montageversicherung (erection cover)** — protects delivered machinery during installation and commissioning at the customer, the window where it's paid for by no one and exposed to everything. *Spot it:* delivery-plus-installation contracts. *Claim example:* A machine is damaged by a rigging slip during commissioning at the customer — €30,000, in the exact gap between the transport cover (ended at delivery) and the customer's property policy (not yet accepted).
- **Photovoltaik cover** — plant-and-yield cover for the hall roof array. *Spot it:* production halls with PV. *Claim example:* Hail wrecks a third of the rooftop array — €40,000 in panels and lost feed-in, a PV-policy claim the building cover would dispute.
- **Kautionsversicherung (surety)** — bonds for supply and framework contracts that demand security. *Spot it:* bond clauses in customer contracts. *How it plays out:* A framework agreement requires a €50,000 performance bond; the surety line issues it and the credit line stays free for raw materials.
- **bAV / Gruppen-Unfall / bKV (benefits)** — pension, accident, and health benefits as the retention package for skilled industrial workers. *Spot it:* machinists taking months to hire. *Claim example:* A machine operator injured in a private motorcycle accident gets the group accident lump sum and keeps his standard of living through recovery — and the story does more for retention than any poster.
- **Betriebsschließungsversicherung (ordered-closure cover)** — pays for authority-ordered closure in food production under the Infection Protection Act. *Spot it:* any food line. *Claim example:* A listeria finding closes production for two weeks of deep cleaning and batch destruction — €60,000 in lost output and disposal; verify the covered-pathogen list in the current wording.

## 16. Agriculture & Direct Marketing
**Typical businesses:** Arable farming, livestock, special crops & orchards, agricultural contractors, farm shops & direct marketing, energy farming (biogas/PV)
**Opening question:** "Your operation is home, machine park, warehouse, and company in one — where does private end and commercial begin for you?"

**Must-have**
- **Agricultural liability (landwirtschaftliche Betriebshaftpflicht)** — pays for the injuries and damage a farm generates by existing: animals that escape, slurry that runs, machines on public roads, visitors on the yard. Animal keeper's liability alone is strict — the keeper is liable for what the animal does regardless of fault. *Spot it:* every farm; verify the wording covers the actual mix (livestock, contracting, visitors, events). *Claim example:* Cattle break through a fence onto the road at dusk; a car hits one and the driver is seriously injured — a €200,000 claim under strict animal-keeper liability, no negligence required. This single scenario justifies the policy.
- **Agricultural contents cover (landwirtschaftliche Inhaltsversicherung)** — replacement value for what the farm holds: harvest, stores, feed, equipment, and livestock within the wording. A farm's wealth sits in its barns, and fire is its oldest enemy. *Spot it:* full stores after harvest, mixed storage of hay and machinery. *Claim example:* A barn fire takes the hay store and two parked machines — €120,000 in one night. The payout matched the loss only because the sums were revalued after harvest; ask when that last happened.
- **Mobile machinery + commercial building cover** — machinery cover for the self-propelled fleet (tractors, harvesters — enormous capital that works outdoors and burns or breaks in season) and building cover for the farm structures against fire, storm, and natural hazards. *Spot it:* every mechanized farm; ask what the combine is worth and what its downtime costs mid-harvest. *Claim example:* A combine catches fire in the field in week one of harvest — €150,000 machine loss plus the scramble for replacement capacity while the crop stands. The machinery policy pays the machine; the building policy would have handled the barn it thankfully wasn't parked in.

**Recommended**
- **Photovoltaik cover** — plant-and-yield insurance for roof and ground-mounted arrays, which for many farms are a revenue line of their own now. *Spot it:* feed-in income on the books, expansion plans. *Claim example:* A hailstorm destroys €40,000 of panels on the barn roof and months of feed-in revenue — a PV-policy claim covering both the hardware and the yield.
- **Firmen-Rechtsschutz** — legal costs for the farm's characteristic fights: lease (Pacht) disputes, neighbor conflicts, subsidy clawbacks, employment. Dedicated agricultural variants exist — check availability. *Spot it:* leased land, subsidy dependence. *Claim example:* A landowner terminates a long-standing Pacht arrangement irregularly; defending the lease through court costs €9,000 in fees — carried by the policy, and the land stays in the rotation.
- **Fixed-cost cover** — pays the running costs after a major loss while the operation rebuilds. *Spot it:* financed operations with debt service. *Claim example:* After the barn fire above, feed purchases, debt service, and wages run on for months before the operation normalizes — €30,000 of continuing costs the fixed-cost cover absorbs.

**Situational**
- **Product liability / closure cover for direct marketing** — the farm shop turns the farmer into a food business, with food-product liability and the exposure to authority-ordered closure. *Spot it:* farm shops, processing, market stalls, online sales. *Claim example:* A batch of raw-milk cheese from the farm dairy is linked to illness; recall, destruction, and a two-week ordered closure of the shop cost €25,000 — claims that live in the product-liability and closure covers, not the basic farm policy. Verify the wording covers the processing and retail activity.
- **Cyberversicherung** — response cover for the farm's digital core: milking robots, feeding systems, barn controls, herd-management data. *Spot it:* automated barns, smart-farming tools. *Claim example:* The herd-management and milking system is encrypted by ransomware; manual operation, recovery, and lost milk-quality data cost €20,000 across a bad week.
- **Gruppen-Unfall / bKV (benefits)** — accident lump sums and health top-ups where the agricultural statutory system stops: leisure time, benefit level, and the contributing family members whose status is often unclear. *Spot it:* family labor, generational handover. *Claim example:* The farmer's son is badly hurt in a private sports accident during his years of working into the operation — statutory benefits are thin, and the group accident lump sum is what bridges the family through it. Crop-yield and livestock-mortality covers are specialty lines with their own market — say so openly and place them separately.

# Part 2 — Spotting the need

German owner-managers rarely volunteer problems, distrust anything that smells like a pitch, and respect demonstrated industry knowledge above all. Discovery is matter-of-fact, document-anchored, and occasion-driven. Five mechanics carry the discipline, then the industry-by-industry cue cards — each cue with the *why* (so you understand the exposure, not just the question), the *pivot* (the question to ask), and the *listen-for* (the phrase that tells you the need is live).

**1. The Berufsgenossenschaft boundary as door-opener**
Every employer already pays statutory accident contributions, and most believe the cover reaches further than it does. Never argue against it — use it as the anchor: it covers employees' workplace and commuting accidents and occupational diseases; it does not cover leisure time (where most accidents happen), it pays no pain-and-suffering-level benefits, and the owner is — depending on the Berufsgenossenschaft — only voluntarily insured or not at all. Line to use: "The Berufsgenossenschaft covers your people's workplace accidents. The question is: what happens to you personally, and what happens Saturday at the football pitch?" The annual contribution notice is also the most natural document for verifying payroll and business description — which surfaces underinsurance and misclassified operations without it feeling like an interrogation.

**2. Occasion-driven advice beats cold pitching**
Business owners buy at life events of the business. The trigger list to monitor and ask about explicitly: founding or master-craftsman exam, relocation or extension, new machine or major investment, first hire, revenue jump, new webshop, PV on the roof, new vehicles, handover to the next generation. Each changes the risk profile and is simultaneously a legitimate, § 6 VVG-compliant reason to advise. Line to use: "What has changed in the business in the last two years — space, people, machines, revenue? The policy from back then knows nothing about any of it."

**3. Documents beat question lists**
An owner answers twenty questions grudgingly but will readily put the current policy, the latest endorsement, or the contribution notice on the table if you offer a concrete review. The underinsurance check — sum insured against today's replacement value, buffer, waiver — is the strongest first-meeting tool: it almost always produces a finding, it is factual, and it documents itself in the advice record.

**4. Listen for these lines — the universal tells**
"Nothing has ever happened here" (risk denial — enter via same-industry claim examples, never via fear). "My tax advisor / my wife / my broker handles that" (you've just learned the decision structure — involve the named third party, don't route around them). "We can't find people" (pivot to bKV, bAV, Gruppen-Unfall as employer positioning). "We have a webshop now / everything runs through the software" (Cyber and Elektronik). "The bank wants a guarantee" (Kautionsversicherung). "The warehouse is twice as full as it used to be" (underinsurance, immediately).

**5. The skilled-labor shortage is the biggest open door**
Employer-benefit lines (bKV, bAV, Gruppen-Unfall) are the one product family where the client brings the pain to you unprompted. Position them as an HR instrument, never as insurance: "This isn't an insurance topic, it's a retention argument for your people that costs less than one replacement hire."

## The cues by industry

### 1. Construction & Finishing Trades — *crews on other people's property, value in the van*
- **Post-handover liability.** *Why:* Trade defects surface long after the invoice is paid, and the claim lands on the firm whether or not the crew still remembers the job — without the completed-work and working-damage clauses, the policy walks away exactly then. *Pivot:* "Who pays when your work causes damage months after acceptance?" *Listen for:* callbacks, warranty visits, "we're in someone's house every day."
- **Tools in transit.** *Why:* The building-based contents policy stops at the workshop door, and a trade firm's capital rides around in vans — the gap is total, not partial. *Pivot:* "What's the replacement value of what's sitting in the vans right now?" *Listen for:* "my whole setup is in the bus," trailers loaded overnight.
- **Bauleistung / Montage.** *Why:* An unfinished structure and a delivered-but-uninstalled system belong to nobody's finished-property policy — the contractor carries the loss until handover. *Pivot:* "Are you on new builds, or installing high-value systems?" *Listen for:* shell construction, delivered units waiting on site.
- **Kaution.** *Why:* Cash retentions and bank guarantees tie up the firm's liquidity for years; insurer-issued bonds release it — this is a financing conversation wearing an insurance label. *Pivot:* "Are clients withholding retentions or demanding guarantees?" *Listen for:* "the bank line is tight," public tenders.

### 2. Cleaning & Facility Services — *keys in hand, inside client space*
- **Key loss & working damage.** *Why:* The two most likely losses in this trade — the lost master key and the damaged surface being cleaned — are exactly the two things basic liability policies restrict or cap. *Pivot:* "Do you hold master keys or alarm codes?" *Listen for:* after-hours access, "we have the master key."
- **Rechtsschutz (employment).** *Why:* High headcount plus high turnover makes the labour court this industry's most probable legal venue, and at first instance each side pays its own lawyer win or lose. *Pivot:* "How many people, and how much turnover in the team?" *Listen for:* dismissal stories, wage disputes.
- **Fleet.** *Why:* Daily multi-site driving turns motor claims into a frequency cost that single policies manage badly and a fleet contract manages well. *Pivot:* "How do the crews move between sites?" *Listen for:* multiple objects serviced per day.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* The scheduling software holds client alarm codes and floor plans — a breach here is a physical-security incident for every client at once. *Pivot:* "Where do access codes and schedules live?" *Listen for:* scheduling apps, client portals.

### 3. Landscaping & Grounds — *machines in the open, duties on other people's ground*
- **Winter service liability.** *Why:* Gritting contracts transfer the property owner's traffic-safety duty to the firm — one icy morning can produce a six-figure injury claim aimed squarely at the contractor. *Pivot:* "Do you plow or grit in winter?" *Listen for:* "snow keeps us busy year-round," seasonal contracts.
- **Mobile machinery.** *Why:* Excavators and mowers work and sleep outdoors where no contents policy reaches, and they're the easiest high-value theft in commercial Germany. *Pivot:* "Does equipment stay on trailers or sites overnight?" *Listen for:* "it all stays on the trailer."
- **Environmental modules.** *Why:* Spray drift and fuel spills produce public-law cleanup orders that standard liability wording excludes — the authority doesn't care whose fault it was. *Pivot:* "Do you apply plant-protection products?" *Listen for:* "we treat lawns," spraying programs.
- **Bauleistung.** *Why:* Walls, paving, and plantings belong to the contractor until handover — weather damage before acceptance is his loss alone. *Pivot:* "Any larger new installations this season?" *Listen for:* tender language, staged projects.

### 4. Food Service & Hospitality — *guests, food, and an authority that can close you*
- **Betriebsschließung.** *Why:* The health authority can close a food business by administrative order, and neither the liability nor the property policy pays a cent for it — this is the one closure risk unique to food. *Pivot:* "What happens if the health office closes you for a week?" *Listen for:* inspection stories, hygiene audits.
- **Contents underinsurance.** *Why:* Hospitality reinvests constantly, so sums insured age faster here than anywhere — and an outdated sum triggers proportional payout cuts exactly when the kitchen burns. *Pivot:* "When did the sum insured last see your kitchen-equipment invoices?" *Listen for:* recent renovation, new kitchen line.
- **Business interruption.** *Why:* Thin margins on high fixed costs mean the closure usually outdamages the fire — rent and payroll don't pause for repairs. *Pivot:* "How long can rent and payroll run on reserves?" *Listen for:* thin-margin talk, high fixed rent.
- **bKV / benefits.** *Why:* In the sector with Germany's worst staffing shortage, a visible health benefit differentiates an employer more per euro than any wage increase. *Pivot:* "How hard is hiring right now?" *Listen for:* "we're closed Mondays because we have no staff."

### 5. Retail — *stock is the capital, footfall is the risk*
- **Seasonal underinsurance.** *Why:* Stock value at the seasonal peak can be double the annual average, and the burglar knows the calendar too — a March sum insured meets a November loss. *Pivot:* "What's on the floor and in the back at the seasonal peak — versus what's in the policy?" *Listen for:* "the warehouse is full for Christmas."
- **Cyber / POS.** *Why:* The till and the webshop are the revenue pipe — when they stop, the shop is open but earning nothing, and card data brings notification duties on top. *Pivot:* "What happens when the till system is down on a Saturday?" *Listen for:* webshop mentions, card-only checkout.
- **Business interruption.** *Why:* An A-location lease keeps billing through the rebuild — the rent is the loss even when the stock is saved. *Pivot:* "Would the landlord pause the rent after a water damage? Neither would ours." *Listen for:* prime-location rent talk.
- **Transport.** *Why:* Goods in the owner's own van between wholesaler and branches are covered by nothing — the contents policy ends at the shop door and motor cover pays only the other party. *Pivot:* "Do goods move between branches or from your own pickups?" *Listen for:* own van runs to the wholesaler.

### 6. Real Estate & Property Management — *other people's money and buildings*
- **Mandatory PI.** *Why:* Managers' and agents' errors destroy money, not property — and professional law makes the cover a license condition, so an outdated level is a compliance gap and a personal-liability gap at once. *Pivot:* "Is your professional indemnity at the level your license requires?" *Listen for:* new WEG mandates, growing units under management.
- **Elementar.** *Why:* Heavy-rain and backflow losses are the fastest-growing building claims in Germany, and the standard building policy pays nothing on them without the natural-hazards module. *Pivot:* "Which buildings in the portfolio have natural-hazards cover?" *Listen for:* heavy-rain stories, basement flooding.
- **Photovoltaik.** *Why:* A PV plant is neither clearly building nor clearly contents — unallocated, both insurers point at each other after the fire. *Pivot:* "Any PV going onto the roofs?" *Listen for:* owner-association resolutions on solar.
- **Rechtsschutz.** *Why:* Tenancy and WEG disputes are the operating noise of this business, and legal costs decide which fights are affordable. *Pivot:* "How many open tenancy disputes right now?" *Listen for:* eviction cases, arrears.

### 7. Professional & Financial Services — *advice is the product, liability is personal*
- **Vermögensschaden-Haftpflicht.** *Why:* When the product is advice, every error is a pure financial loss — the one category general liability explicitly excludes and, for licensed professions, the one the law requires covered. *Pivot:* "What happens if advice turns out demonstrably wrong and the client quantifies it?" *Listen for:* growing mandate sizes, new licensed activities.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* Client data is the entire relationship; a leak is simultaneously a GDPR case, a liability case, and a reputation event — and only funded, professional response contains all three. *Pivot:* "Where does client data live, and who can reach it?" *Listen for:* cloud tools, home-office setups.
- **Rechtsschutz.** *Why:* Advisory firms write off unpaid fees because suing feels expensive — legal-expenses cover converts write-offs back into receivables. *Pivot:* "Ever had a fee go unpaid and thought about court?" *Listen for:* receivables complaints.
- **Benefits.** *Why:* In a people business the assets leave every evening — pension and health benefits are the cheapest way to make them come back. *Pivot:* "How do you keep good people from being poached?" *Listen for:* hiring frustration.

### 8. IT & Digital Services — *your error becomes the client's downtime*
- **IT professional indemnity.** *Why:* Software errors don't break things, they stop things — and a stopped client production line is a pure financial loss only the IT-PI wording covers. *Pivot:* "If your update halts their production, who pays the standstill?" *Listen for:* SLAs, go-live dates, managed-services contracts.
- **Own cyber cover.** *Why:* An IT firm's breach cascades into every client it manages, and a provider that can't fund its own incident response loses its credibility asset overnight. *Pivot:* "Would your clients stay if you were the one breached?" *Listen for:* admin access to client systems.
- **Elektronik.** *Why:* The firm's production plant is its hardware, and the ways hardware actually dies — surge, drop, theft — are exactly what contents policies exclude. *Pivot:* "What's the hardware worth in the office and the rack?" *Listen for:* own server room, dev workstations.
- **Rechtsschutz.** *Why:* Acceptance and scope disputes are this sector's signature conflict, and they're won with funded lawyers, not email threads. *Pivot:* "Any disputes over project acceptance?" *Listen for:* "the client won't sign off."

### 9. Agencies, Marketing & Media — *published work, third-party rights*
- **Media liability / PI.** *Why:* Everything an agency ships is publication, and publication risk — image rights, trademarks, personality rights — is pure financial loss that only a media-aware wording carries. *Pivot:* "Who clears image, music, and trademark rights — and who pays when it goes wrong?" *Listen for:* stock-photo use, influencer campaigns.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* Admin access to client ad accounts plus authority over client budgets means one phished login spends someone else's money — and the client will want it back from the agency. *Pivot:* "Do you hold client ad accounts and budgets?" *Listen for:* "we run their whole performance spend."
- **Elektronik.** *Why:* Production gear earns on location and gets stolen from vans — precisely where the office contents policy no longer applies. *Pivot:* "What travels to a shoot in one van?" *Listen for:* camera lists, rented gear.
- **Rechtsschutz.** *Why:* Usage-rights and fee fights arrive after the relationship ends, when goodwill is gone and only funded enforcement works. *Pivot:* "Ever fought over usage rights after a project ended?" *Listen for:* scope-creep stories.

### 10. Healthcare & Care Services — *bodily contact plus the most sensitive data*
- **Professional liability.** *Why:* Hands-on treatment makes bodily-injury allegations statistically inevitable, and the defense of the unfounded allegation costs as much as the payout of the founded one. *Pivot:* "Who defends the treatment-error allegation — justified or not?" *Listen for:* new treatment methods, delegated staff treatments.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* Patient records are health data — the GDPR's most protected category — and a locked practice system stops treatment, scheduling, and billing simultaneously. *Pivot:* "What happens if the practice software is encrypted on a Monday?" *Listen for:* digital patient files, online booking.
- **Fixed-cost cover.** *Why:* Practices bill per session; a closed room means zero revenue from day one while rent, salaries, and device leasing continue in full. *Pivot:* "If the practice stands still four weeks, what keeps getting billed to you?" *Listen for:* high fit-out financing.
- **Fleet.** *Why:* For outpatient care the vehicles are the service — a dozen small cars on daily tours generate monthly motor claims and can't afford a day without a replacement vehicle. *Pivot:* "How many cars are on tour daily?" *Listen for:* route plans, staff drivers.

### 11. Personal Care & Fitness — *hands on the client, all day*
- **Treatment/training injuries.** *Why:* The injury here happens *through* the service — chemicals on skin, loads on bodies — so the treatment-damage clause is the policy's heart, and new service lines outrun old wordings. *Pivot:* "What if a coloring, a treatment, or a session causes an injury?" *Listen for:* new offerings (chemical peels, EMS training).
- **Contents + BI.** *Why:* These businesses are their rooms — no studio, no revenue — while rent and leased equipment keep billing through the rebuild. *Pivot:* "The studio burns Saturday night — when do you reopen, and who pays the rent meanwhile?" *Listen for:* fresh renovations, leased equipment.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* Booking and subscription systems hold members' identity and payment kits, and a small studio's breach response is unaffordable without cover. *Pivot:* "Where do member and payment data sit?" *Listen for:* booking apps, subscription billing.
- **Benefits.** *Why:* Trained staff can open next door with a chair and a mirror — retention benefits are cheaper than the revenue they walk out with. *Pivot:* "How do you keep trained people from opening next door?" *Listen for:* churn complaints.

### 12. Automotive Trades — *custody of customer vehicles*
- **Custody damage.** *Why:* Standard liability excludes damage to property in the firm's custody — which in this trade is the entire business model, every car on the lot. *Pivot:* "Whose policy pays for the customer's car on your lot overnight — or on the test drive?" *Listen for:* full yards, dealer plates.
- **Environmental.** *Why:* Oils, fuels, and batteries produce public-law cleanup orders when they escape — payable regardless of fault and excluded from basic wordings. *Pivot:* "How are oils, fuels, and batteries stored and disposed of?" *Listen for:* EV work, tank talk.
- **Contents / machinery.** *Why:* Diagnostics and lifts are the earning capacity, they're theft magnets, and machinery fails from within in ways fire policies never cover. *Pivot:* "What would it cost to re-equip the workshop from zero?" *Listen for:* new diagnostics, calibration rigs.
- **Rechtsschutz.** *Why:* Disputed invoices are this trade's permanent weather, and the expert report that wins them costs more than most invoices. *Pivot:* "How often do repair invoices get disputed?" *Listen for:* warranty arguments.

### 13. Education & Training — *supervision duty, often over minors*
- **Supervision liability.** *Why:* Providers stand in for parents' duty of care, and every incident involving a minor arrives with a supervision allegation attached — the defense is the product here. *Pivot:* "Who is liable if a child is hurt during a lesson or on an excursion?" *Listen for:* group sizes, off-site activities.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* Children's data plus parents' payment details is the combination with zero public tolerance for sloppy handling. *Pivot:* "Where do participant data and course platforms run?" *Listen for:* online offerings, payment plans.
- **Vermögensschaden-Haftpflicht.** *Why:* Wrong registration advice or a botched qualification pathway costs a participant money and time — pure financial loss the liability policy excludes. *Pivot:* "Do you certify outcomes or prepare people for examinations?" *Listen for:* promised qualifications.
- **Gruppen-Unfall.** *Why:* Statutory pupil accident cover applies to school contexts, not private courses — so participant accident cover is both a real gap and a visible quality marker. *Pivot:* "What accident cover do participants have with you — and do parents ask?" *Listen for:* enrollment-form questions.

### 14. Events & Entertainment — *value that travels, crowds that gather*
- **Gear in transit.** *Why:* Event equipment earns on the road and in halls — exactly where the stationary contents policy has already stopped applying — and the theft happens the night before the show. *Pivot:* "Where does the rig sleep the night before the show?" *Listen for:* loaded vans at motorway hotels.
- **Organizer liability.** *Why:* Crowd and structure risk isn't automatically in a business liability policy, and tariffs cap the event sizes they accept — growth into bigger formats silently outruns the cover. *Pivot:* "What's the biggest crowd you've had — and the biggest you're bidding for?" *Listen for:* growth into larger formats.
- **Montage.** *Why:* Build-up and tear-down is where structures fall and venues get damaged, in a deliberate boundary zone between erection cover and liability. *Pivot:* "Do you build stages or stands yourselves?" *Listen for:* rigging, trade-fair projects.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* An own ticket shop means holding thousands of card transactions for strangers — a breach is a mass-notification event with refunds attached. *Pivot:* "Do you run the ticketing and hold attendee data?" *Listen for:* own shop systems.

### 15. Light Manufacturing & Wholesale — *one machine, one recall, one bottleneck*
- **Product liability / recall.** *Why:* Once a product carries the firm's name, downstream failures travel back up the chain — and recall logistics hit even the faultless supplier, at costs basic wordings don't carry. *Pivot:* "What happens if one batch has to come back from the market?" *Listen for:* private-label production, supplying into larger brands.
- **Machinery + BI.** *Why:* Machines fail from within — no fire, no burglary, no property trigger — and the standstill behind the bottleneck machine usually costs several times the repair. *Pivot:* "Which single machine's failure stops everything?" *Listen for:* "everything runs through that line."
- **Transport.** *Why:* In wholesale, the second inventory is permanently on the road or on the water, where the warehouse contents policy has never applied. *Pivot:* "Who carries the risk on inbound and outbound goods?" *Listen for:* Incoterms talk, own delivery fleet.
- **Cyber.** *Why:* Ransomware in the ERP is a full production standstill without any property trigger — the modern interruption that property policies structurally can't see. *Pivot:* "Is production planning digital — and what happens when it's encrypted?" *Listen for:* ERP and warehouse-management systems.

### 16. Agriculture & Direct Marketing — *farm, home, and firm in one yard*
- **Agricultural liability.** *Why:* Animal-keeper liability is strict — the keeper pays for what the animal does, fault or no fault — and farms generate liability by existing: roads, visitors, emissions. *Pivot:* "Animals, machines on public roads, visitors on the yard — where does your policy draw the line?" *Listen for:* new branches (farm events, boarding animals).
- **Contents & machinery values.** *Why:* A farm's wealth sits in barns and machines whose values swing with the harvest calendar — sums insured set in spring meet fires in autumn. *Pivot:* "When were harvest, stores, and machine park last revalued?" *Listen for:* new harvest technology, full stores.
- **Photovoltaik / energy.** *Why:* Roof and land arrays are a second business line with their own hardware and yield risk, sitting awkwardly between farm and building policies unless placed deliberately. *Pivot:* "Is the roof or the land earning yet?" *Listen for:* feed-in talk, biogas plans.
- **Direct marketing.** *Why:* The farm shop makes the farmer a food business — with product liability and the health authority's closure power that the basic farm policy never contemplated. *Pivot:* "Does the farm shop make you a food business?" *Listen for:* processing, markets, online sales.

# Part 3 — Objection handling

**Five ground rules before the individual objections:**

1. **Price is the real objection less often than it sounds.** The dominant objection in German commercial is attachment: to the existing advisor, to running contracts with notice periods, to habit. Trust and relationship tenure beat premium.
2. **Hard closing destroys the conversation.** Pressure, artificial scarcity, and aggressive closing formulas activate a deep-seated distrust of insurance salespeople inherited from commission-driven structured sales. The close is the logical consequence of a clean, documented analysis — not a moment of overcoming resistance.
3. **Written follow-up is a friend, not an evasion.** "Send it to me in writing" is often a genuine process step, not a brush-off. Deliver it structured and fix a follow-up date, and you gain credibility from it. Without the fixed date, it was a brush-off after all — the date is the test.
4. **Notice periods structure the sales motion.** Annual contracts with three-month notice mean many conversations correctly end with a date in the calendar rather than a signature. A managed renewal-date and follow-up system is not the consolation prize — it *is* the pipeline.
5. **The advice record is a sales instrument.** The documented sentence "client was informed of coverage gap X and declined" focuses decision-makers like almost no other argument — never play it as a threat, present it as lived diligence.

**The objections, ranked by frequency:**

### 1. "My agent/broker handles all of that — I've been with him for 20 years." *(Incumbent / the most common objection)*
- **What they're really saying:** Loyalty and convenience, not verified coverage quality. Twenty years of relationship often means twenty-year-old sums insured.
- **Reframe:** "That speaks well of you — good service is worth keeping, and I'm not here to displace anyone. The only question is: when was it last checked whether the contracts still match today's business? Companies change faster than policies."
- **Bridge:** "When did someone last hold your sums insured against today's stock value and payroll? That's exactly what I'm offering — no obligation, in writing, and if everything fits, I'll tell you exactly that."
- **Play:** Never attack the incumbent — it loses. Offer the factual second opinion with a documented result, and be transparent about your own role and mandate; that transparency pays straight into trust.

### 2. "I'm already insured through the Berufsgenossenschaft." *(Statutory/private confusion)*
- **What they're really saying:** The most widespread coverage illusion in the market — a statutory employer obligation mistaken for comprehensive protection. Not resistance; a knowledge gap.
- **Reframe:** "Correct, and you need it. The Berufsgenossenschaft covers your employees' workplace accidents. It covers neither your liability toward customers, nor your equipment, nor you as the owner — those are three completely separate construction sites."
- **Bridge:** "Let's lay out cleanly what you already have by law and where genuinely nothing is in place — then we only talk about the real gaps."
- **Play:** Never pitch anything the client holds by statute. Handling that boundary cleanly — here and with motor liability — builds instant competence trust and separates you from every salesperson they've met before.

### 3. "Send me something in writing first." *(Stall / culture)*
- **What they're really saying:** Partly a genuine review wish, partly a polite brush-off. The difference reveals itself in whether a date gets attached.
- **Reframe:** "Gladly — that's part of my process anyway; you'll get the analysis in writing and traceable. So the paper is actually worth something to you: which two or three points should definitely be in it?"
- **Bridge:** "I'll send it by Thursday. Does early next week work — fifteen minutes to walk through the two flagged points?"
- **Play:** Don't push back on the written form — deliver it, structure it, and fix the follow-up in the same breath. The document plus the kept deadline *is* the sales argument.

### 4. "My contract still runs until..." *(Notice period)*
- **What they're really saying:** Usually simply true. Annual contracts with three-month notice are the norm.
- **Reframe:** "Completely fine — which makes now exactly the right time: we review calmly, without pressure, and if it fits better, we pre-book the renewal date. And some things have no incumbent at all: a cyber policy or a group accident cover you can arrange today, independent of it."
- **Bridge:** "When is your main renewal date? I'll set our review three months before it — and until then we close the gaps that are already open today."
- **Play:** The answer is a system, not rhetoric: renewal-date list, follow-up discipline, cancellation support — plus consistently selling the incumbent-free gaps (cyber, legal expenses, benefits) ahead of the renewal date.

### 5. "Let me think about it." *(Stall)*
- **What they're really saying:** Usually one unspoken concern rather than a real need for time — though sometimes genuine deliberation. The two are distinguishable: real deliberation accepts a concrete follow-up date; the brush-off avoids one.
- **Reframe:** "Of course — it's your business and it deserves consideration. In my experience, when someone wants to think it over, one specific point isn't settled yet. Which one is it for you — the cover, the cost, or the timing?"
- **Bridge:** "What information would make the decision easy for you? I'll bring exactly that to the follow-up — does Tuesday work?"
- **Play:** Respect genuine deliberation instead of trying to break it. The winning combination is the same as for the written-form objection: a structured summary plus a fixed date. A scheduled second conversation converts better than a pushed first one.

### 6. "Too expensive." / "I saw it cheaper elsewhere." *(Price)*
- **What they're really saying:** Premium viewed in isolation, not against the risk and almost never against the quality of the conditions. Comparison-portal prices rarely compare equal cover.
- **Reframe:** "I understand — more expensive than what, exactly? Than another quote, or than what you'd budgeted? And on the other quote: same waiver of underinsurance, same cover for damage to the property being worked on? That's exactly where the differences sit — the ones you only see at claim time."
- **Bridge:** "What would a single uninsured loss from your own trade cost you — the water damage after handover, the master key? That's the number the premium competes against, not the portal price."
- **Play:** Never answer with a discount — it damages your advisor position. The conditions argument wins: replacement value, buffer, waiver of underinsurance, and — where the tariff includes one — a conditions-update guarantee that keeps the contract from aging. Name the feature, confirm the detail in the current conditions.

### 7. "I need to ask my tax advisor first." *(Authority)*
- **What they're really saying:** The Steuerberater is the most trusted external advisor in the Mittelstand. Not an excuse — the real decision structure, and an opportunity.
- **Reframe:** "Good idea, he knows your numbers. So he can assess it quickly, I'll summarize the recommendation with reasoning on one page — deductibility, payroll linkage, all on it. On bAV and bKV he'll want a say anyway."
- **Bridge:** "Shall we set the follow-up so you have his okay by then — or bring him in directly on the benefits topics?"
- **Play:** Never route around the tax advisor — equip and involve him. On bAV and bKV he is a de-facto co-decider, and a convinced Steuerberater refers you onward. Leave tax statements cleanly to him: no tax advice from the intermediary.

### 8. "Doesn't my Betriebshaftpflicht already cover all of that?" *(Coverage assumption)*
- **What they're really saying:** The liability policy mistaken for all-round protection. The standard confusions: own stock and equipment (needs contents cover), own downtime (business interruption), pure financial loss (Vermögensschaden-Haftpflicht), cyber, and the legal costs of the firm's own disputes (Rechtsschutz).
- **Reframe:** "The Betriebshaftpflicht pays when you damage *others*. Everything that happens *to you* — your stock, your downtime, your data, your own legal disputes — is a different policy. It's the foundation, not the roof."
- **Bridge:** "Shall we sort your risks into two buckets — damage you cause versus damage you suffer? Then you see in one view what the liability policy carries and what currently nothing carries."
- **Play:** The two-bucket explanation (liability outward vs. own losses inward) is the cleanest framing there is, and it works across every industry in Part 1. Draw it on paper — it survives the meeting.

### 9. "Nothing has happened here in 30 years." *(Risk denial)*
- **What they're really saying:** Lived experience beats statistics — understandable and internally consistent.
- **Reframe:** "And that speaks for your business. Only: you don't insure the past, you insure the one year that goes differently. The colleague with the water damage also had 25 good years before it."
- **Bridge:** "Which loss would genuinely hit your business — not the scratch, but the one that takes six months to recover from? That's the only one we'll talk about; the rest we leave out."
- **Play:** Fear scenarios and drama repel. What works is sober, same-industry claim examples — ideally regional — and the visible willingness to leave out what's unnecessary.

### 10. "I don't want to be over-insured — you people always just want to sell." *(Trust)*
- **What they're really saying:** The legacy of commission-driven structured sales. The client is protecting himself from the salesperson, not from the insurance.
- **Reframe:** "I understand the objection, and it's legitimate — which is why I document every recommendation with reasoning in the advice record, and I document just as clearly what you *don't* need. If I name two things today you can save yourself, will you take seriously the one gap I genuinely mean?"
- **Bridge:** "Let's start with what can go or is duplicated — after that we talk about what's missing."
- **Play:** The statutory advice and documentation duty is the strongest anti-distrust tool you have. Deselect first, recommend second — the order is the message.

### 11. "Insurers never pay when it matters anyway." *(Trust)*
- **What they're really saying:** An experienced or retold claims disappointment — almost always caused by underinsurance, breached policyholder duties (Obliegenheiten), or a never-reviewed policy.
- **Reframe:** "Those stories exist, and almost always the same thing is behind them: the sum was too low, or a duty was breached, and nobody knew either beforehand. That's exactly why we check it *before* the claim — the waiver of underinsurance and the duties you need to keep are in my record, not in the fine print."
- **Bridge:** "May I show you, on your current policy, the two or three places where it typically jams at claim time?"
- **Play:** Underinsurance with proportional reduction (Quotelung) is the most common real-world reason for cut payouts — which makes the underinsurance check the direct answer to this objection. Explain Obliegenheiten and the § 19 VVG disclosure duty proactively; transparency about the rules of the game is the sales argument.

### 12. "Money's tight right now." *(Price / cash flow)*
- **What they're really saying:** Often a real liquidity constraint, not a negotiating move. Treat it as a prioritization signal.
- **Reframe:** "Then we should be doubly careful that a single loss doesn't hit exactly that tight liquidity. Let's do triage: which risk would break the business, and which would merely annoy it? We cover the first category and consciously park the rest — documented."
- **Bridge:** "If we close only the one existential gap today and put the rest on the renewal review — which gap would that be for you?"
- **Play:** No discounts. Check payment-cadence options in the current tariff rather than promising them. Record the conscious deferral in the advice record: the documented "reviewed and postponed" protects the client, protects you under § 61 VVG, and creates the legitimate re-entry point.

### 13. "My business is too small for all that." *(Cross-sell)*
- **What they're really saying:** Covers like cyber, legal expenses, or business interruption perceived as corporate topics.
- **Reframe:** "The other way round: the large company absorbs the loss from petty cash — for you, the whole business hangs on the same loss. That's exactly why compact SME tariffs exist: cut for your size."
- **Bridge:** "How many weeks of standstill can your account absorb before it gets serious? That's the only number that decides 'too small'."
- **Play:** Point to the compact SME product lines as visible proof the cover was built for this size of business — then keep the recommendation to the one or two covers that map to an existential loss, not a catalogue.

### 14. "Cyber doesn't concern us — we're not an IT company." *(Cross-sell / the most common product-specific objection)*
- **What they're really saying:** Cyber read as a technology topic, not as a business-interruption and payment-fraud topic.
- **Reframe:** "Attackers aren't looking for IT companies, they're looking for open doors — and the invoice with the altered bank details hits the trade business exactly like the law firm. The question isn't whether you sell IT; it's whether your business runs for a week without email, till, and bookkeeping."
- **Bridge:** "What concretely happens at your firm if no computer opens tomorrow morning and the last outgoing invoice was paid to the wrong account?"
- **Play:** The effective entry runs through business interruption and payment-diversion fraud, not through data-protection theory; GDPR notification duties are the factual supporting argument, not the lead.

### 15. "I'll add the rest later." *(Cross-sell deferral)*
- **What they're really saying:** Agreement in principle, deferral in practice — and "later" never arrives without a trigger. The notice-period rhythm makes "later" feel especially legitimate.
- **Reframe:** "Fine — but between now and later, the gap is real. Let's do two things: sort what genuinely can wait from what can't, and put 'later' in the calendar. An open-ended 'later' is a friendly no, and neither of us needs that."
- **Bridge:** "Which date do we write down for the second tranche — and is there anything on the list that would keep you up at night before then?"
- **Play:** Anchor "later" to the concrete renewal date and enter the deferral in the advice record. A dated, documented commitment gets kept in a way a verbal "we'll circle back" never is.

### 16. "Now's not a good time — come back after the season." *(Stall)*
- **What they're really saying:** For seasonal businesses (hospitality, landscaping, events, retail) often simply true — and simultaneously risky, because peak season is peak exposure.
- **Reframe:** "Precisely because this is your strongest period, it's also your biggest risk — full warehouse, full order book, full floor. I don't need a decision on everything today: just the one gap that hurts during the season, and a firm date afterwards for the rest."
- **Bridge:** "Which week after season-end shall I book firmly — and until then, may I at least have the sum insured adjusted for the full warehouse?"
- **Play:** Resolve it through a firmly agreed, documented follow-up date rather than forcing a first-contact close. A dated "no, but in November" is, as a rule, kept — treat it as pipeline, not as a loss.

---

**Bottom line on objection work:** Four objections set the direction of most conversations — incumbent attachment, the statutory-cover confusion, the written-form preference, and contract notice periods. Master those four, run a renewal-date system, and use the advice record visibly as proof of diligence, and you need no closing technique. The rest is conditions quality: replacement value, premium-free buffer, waiver of underinsurance — name the feature, confirm the number in the current conditions.
